About

I'm a junior at UNC Charlotte studying Mechanical Engineering with an Aerospace concentration, graduating in spring 2028. I'm currently learning CFD, simulation, and analysis through the FSAE EV aerodynamics team and my own projects, building on the propulsion and recovery work I did with the Rocketry Club last year.

Before English, Spanish, or Portuguese, my first language was math. My mom homeschooled me in our one-bedroom in Maryland and made it a point to fit as many languages and as much algebra into my head as she could, before I even realized what I was learning. By the time I started kindergarten, I'd made it to the quotient rule.

Growing up between cultures taught me to be nobody but myself, not what's expected of me because of how I look or where I come from.

I can't pinpoint where the urge to reach for the stars came from, literally or figuratively. I just knew from a young age that I wanted to be more, and that it had something to do with the sky. That feeling grew into a love for learning. Languages, instruments, chess: anything with a clear path to get better made me want to get better, and faster. It has followed me into nearly everything I do, along with an ambition to set myself apart in all the ways that matter.

That's also why, outside of engineering, I'm usually inside reading, watching a movie, playing chess, or practicing guitar, and I'm gearing up to learn piano next. Fueled by Chester's Hot Fries and half a dozen eggs a day.

OUR Scholars · Apr to Jun 2026

Adversarial Vulnerability of Deep Learning Models for CNC Tool Wear Classification

Can an attacker fool the AI that decides when a cutting tool is worn out, without touching the machine or changing anything a person can see? I built an attack pipeline in MATLAB against a pre-trained EfficientNet-B0 and benchmarked five attack methods.

  • Noise of 0.004, under 1 pixel value out of 255, dropped accuracy from 92.5% to 17.5%
  • One universal perturbation fooled 82.5% of images in a single pass
  • Attacking only 33.8% of pixels, all on the cutting edge, matched a full-image attack
Advisor: Dr. Ankit Agarwal · MATLAB · EfficientNet-B0

Propulsion & CFD · Oct 2026

Solid Rocket Motor Nozzle

I designed a sea-level nozzle for a Cesaroni F29 motor, then made the CFD prove it. Cesaroni doesn't publish chamber pressure, so I backed it out of the certified thrust curve in Python, matching rated total impulse within 1%. From there, 1D compressible-flow theory sized it: 2.93 mm throat, 4.45 expansion ratio at 400 psi.

  • Modeled a 15° cone and an 80% Rao bell in SolidWorks and ran both in Flow Simulation, accelerating the gas up to Mach 2.7.
  • It did not work the first time. The first run died at iteration 137 on a 14k-cell mesh, with the solver flagging a blown Mach number limit and a vortex crossing the inlet boundary.
  • Two problems, two fixes. The mesh was far too coarse at the throat to catch the jump to supersonic, so I refined it from 14k to 333k cells. I'd also set total pressure on both ends of the domain, which leaves the flow nowhere sensible to go. Switching the outlet to environment pressure fixed that. After both, every goal converged by iteration 3,765.
  • The nozzle delivers 97% of ideal performance (Isp 209 to 211 s vs. 216 s, discharge coefficient 0.96). The real motor only gets 181 s, and that gap is combustion losses, not the nozzle.
  • Bell and cone landed within 1.3% of each other at this size, so I'd machine the cone. Nobody needs to pay for a Rao contour on an F motor.

Avionics · Sep 2026

Flight Computer PCB

My first PCB: a 2-layer flight computer with an ESP32-C3, MPU6050 IMU, BMP280 barometer, AMS1117 regulator, and USB-C power. Routed by hand with zero unrouted nets, plus a 5 mm keep-out under the antenna so the Wi-Fi actually works. Its firmware logs flight data at 100 Hz for glider flight testing.

Hours of tutorials did not prepare me for KiCad. I restarted the layout more than once. I also saved the design rule check for the very end and got every error at once. I run DRC as I go now.

Propulsion · Oct 2026

Turbojet Cycle Analysis

I wanted to know where a jet engine's fuel actually goes, so I built one in Python, station by station from the inlet to the nozzle. Ideal cycle first, then real losses, then altitude, speed, and an afterburner. Then I checked it against a real engine, the GE J85-21.

  • Within 11% on dry thrust and 8% on afterburner fuel use against the J85. The model runs optimistic, which it should: it skips turbine cooling air and bleed.
  • Real losses drop the best compressor pressure ratio from about 16 to 10. Squeezing harder stops paying off a lot sooner than the textbook says.
  • Going from ideal to real costs 30% more fuel at cruise, and the biggest piece isn't a component. Hot combustion gas just takes more energy to heat than air.
  • My first loss breakdown said every loss was about 1%. It was wrong. Adding realism one step at a time fixed it.
Python · NumPy · pytest · validated against Mattingly closed-form solutions
